Already agreed

  • City of Ithaca: $4 million. A year, adjusted for inflation, through June 2039.
  • Ithaca schools: $700,000. 2025–26, rising to $874,000 by 2030–31.
  • Town of Ithaca: $425,000. A year, through June 2034.

Together, about $5.1 million at those published amounts. The city’s figure is the base, before its inflation adjustment. The agreements stay as they are.

Yale’s payment to New Haven is nearly $30 million a year. Princeton’s to its municipality was $5.9 million in 2025.

The plant the campus uses

Cornell has its own drinking-water plant. It does not have its own wastewater plant. Its environmental office says the university would not be able to operate without the Ithaca Area Wastewater Treatment Facility. Labs, dorms, and dining halls discharge there.

Nearly 11,000 Cornell employees live in Tompkins County and the counties next to it. That count is for the year ending June 30, 2023. In that same year Cornell paid $2.6 million in water, sewer, and stormwater fees. Fees are not this capital work.

The recipient would be the Ithaca Area Wastewater Treatment Facility, owned by the City of Ithaca, the Town of Ithaca, and the Town of Dryden. Common Council is scheduled to hear the capital plan on October 20, discuss it October 28, and vote the budget November 4. The published project is about $37.8 million in planned improvements. How much of that is still unfunded is not published. A further voluntary gift, restricted to the plant, would sit on top of the agreements. It would not fund salaries or the city’s operating budget.

Questions people ask

Doesn’t Cornell already pay?
Yes. About $5.1 million a year to the city, the town, and the schools, under agreements that run into the 2030s. That is the black dot above. For comparison, Yale pays New Haven nearly $30 million a year.
Doesn’t Cornell pay for the sewer through its bills?
It pays fees: $2.6 million in water, sewer, and stormwater fees in the year ending June 30, 2023. Fees cover use. They are not the $37.8 million in planned improvements to the plant.
Why the wastewater plant?
Because Cornell’s own environmental office says the university would not be able to operate without it. Campus has its own drinking-water plant, but no wastewater plant.
Would the money just go into the city budget?
No. A further voluntary gift, restricted to the plant, would not fund salaries or the city’s operating budget. The existing agreements stay as they are.
